# What makes a game fun

## Frameworks worth keeping

**MDA (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek).** Designers build *mechanics* (rules), which produce *dynamics*
(what happens when players use them), which give *aesthetics* (what the player feels). Players meet
the game from the feelings end; designers can only touch the rules. So: decide the feeling first,
then ask which rules make it happen, then test whether they do.

**Eight kinds of fun (LeBlanc).** Sensation (it feels good), fantasy (being someone else), narrative
(a story unfolds), challenge (overcoming), fellowship (with others), discovery (finding new things),
expression (making your own mark), submission (pastime, relaxing). A sandbox like PixelRealm lives
on discovery, expression and challenge, with sensation as the glue. Name the two or three a
feature serves; a feature that serves none is clutter.

**Fun is learning (Koster, *A Theory of Fun*).** The brain enjoys finding patterns. A game stays
fun while there is a pattern left to master; once mastered, it is boring. So a long game needs a
steady supply of new patterns: new enemies that break old tactics, new materials, new places with
new rules. Grinding is the game asking you to repeat a pattern you have already learned.

**Flow (Csikszentmihalyi).** Players are happiest when challenge rises with skill: too hard is
anxiety, too easy is boredom. Use a sawtooth: a ramp of rising difficulty, a peak (a boss, a storm,
a deep cave), then a drop to an easier stretch with new tools, then the next ramp.

**Self-determination theory (Ryan, Rigby; PENS).** Lasting motivation comes from *competence*
(I am getting better), *autonomy* (my choices matter) and *relatedness* (I matter to someone or
something: pets, a home, other players). Extrinsic rewards (points, badges) can push out intrinsic
fun if they become the reason to play (the overjustification effect): reward the play, don't
replace it.

**Player motivations (Quantic Foundry).** Measured clusters: action (destruction, excitement),
social (competition, community), mastery (challenge, strategy), achievement (completion, power),
immersion (fantasy, story), creativity (design, discovery). Better than Bartle's four types, which
were drawn from one MUD and should not be used as a survey. Know which clusters your game feeds.

**Interesting decisions (Sid Meier).** A game is a series of interesting decisions. A decision is
interesting when no option is always best, the player has enough information to choose, and the
result is felt. Bad decisions: obvious ones, blind ones, and ones whose results never show.

**Emergence and progression (Juul).** Emergent games give few rules that combine into many
situations (chess, Minecraft); progression games give a sequence made by hand (adventure games).
Sandbox games are emergent at heart and use progression (bosses, tiers, places) to give direction.

## Lessons from long-running designers (Rosewater, *Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons*)

- Fun is not the only goal; resonance (it reminds the player of something they know) is powerful.
- Make use of what players already know; the familiar is easier to learn.
- Design around human nature, not against it: if players do something "wrong", the game invited it.
- The fun part should also be the right strategy; if the best way to win is boring, players will be bored.
- Restrictions breed creativity; give yourself constraints.
- Aim for some players to love it rather than everyone to like it.
- Players are good at telling you something is wrong and bad at telling you how to fix it.
- Aesthetics matter: looks change how players judge the rules.

## What "fun" breaks down into, for a checklist

- **A clear goal** the player chose or at least accepts.
- **Something to learn** (a pattern, a system, a place).
- **Meaningful choices** with costs.
- **Feedback** that is quick, clear and pleasant.
- **Fairness**: failure the player understands and could have avoided.
- **Variety** in rhythm: tension and rest, big and small, fast and slow.
- **Ownership**: something that is theirs (a base, a build, a character, a story).
- **Surprise**: the game occasionally does something the player did not predict but believes.

## Sandbox specifics (Minecraft, Terraria, Stardew Valley)

- Short days give natural deadlines; the first night is the first real goal.
- Bosses and tiers give a sandbox direction without taking away freedom (Terraria's boss gates and
  hardmode switch turn the whole world new again).
- Bounded, varied tasks per day (Stardew): some take a minute, some a week; there is always a short
  one to fit in.
- The home is safe; leaving it is a choice to take risk. Coming home with loot is a reward in itself.
