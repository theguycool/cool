# Onboarding and teaching

The best tutorial is a level where the player can't help learning.

## Principles

- **Do, then explain.** Let the player act within the first seconds. Explanations come after the
  first success, if at all.
- **Teach one thing at a time, when it's needed** (just in time), in a safe place.
- **Never call it a tutorial** (Plants vs. Zombies): it's the first day.
- **Few words**: at most about eight words on screen at a time; a picture of the button beats a
  sentence about it.
- **Introduce, develop, twist, test** (the kishotenketsu structure of Nintendo levels): show the
  idea safely, use it in a harder way, combine it with something else, then test it.
- **Let the world teach**: a gap that can only be jumped, an ore that glints, a cave mouth that
  draws the eye. The first screen of Super Mario Bros. teaches with layout, not text.
- **Keep teaching late**: every new system gets its own small first time.
- **Make it skippable** for players who know.

## Hints

- Wait before hinting; let players try. Offer a hint after a stall, not at once.
- Graduated hints: first a nudge (a sound, a glint), then a pointer, then the answer.
- Contextual tips: shown the first time a situation happens (first night, first ore, first storm),
  once, and kept in a journal to read again.
- Assist modes (Super Guide, Celeste's assist) let players get past a wall without shame.

## Goals

- Always show one next step and why it matters ("Make a workbench: it lets you build tools").
- Goals should point ahead: each recipe or item should hint at what it leads to.
- A first-night goal gives the first session shape: shelter, light, food before dark.

## The first ten minutes, checklist

- Is the player doing the main verb within 30 seconds?
- Do they get a reward within the first minute?
- Do they know their next goal at all times?
- Is anything shown that they don't need yet? Hide it.
- Is there a first danger they can survive and learn from?
- Do they end the first session with a plan for the next?
